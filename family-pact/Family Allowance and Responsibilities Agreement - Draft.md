# Family Allowance and Responsibilities Agreement

Drafted: 25 July 2026

## Purpose

This system is designed to help Kit and Obie:

- learn to manage money and screen time
- contribute properly to a shared home
- take responsibility for clothing, appearance, tidiness and routines
- reduce daily arguments and repeated reminders

This is not a system for paying them for every basic task. Being helpful, tidy and respectful is part of family life. The allowance is to support growing independence and budgeting.

## Proposed Start Date

Start on Monday 3 August 2026 and review after 6 weeks on Sunday 13 September 2026.

## Monthly Allowance

### Base Allowance Formula

Base allowance is:

- `age x 2.5 per month`, rounded to the nearest `£5`

Using their next birthdays:

- Kit at 16: `£40 per month`
- Obie at 14: `£35 per month`

This is a base amount for budgeting.

If they want more money, they can earn it through paid extra jobs.

## What Their Allowance Must Cover

Each child is expected to pay for the following from their allowance:

- everyday non-school clothing choices
- haircuts
- casual shoes or trainers if wanted beyond essentials agreed by parents
- snacks, sweets and impulse buys
- small social spending
- presents for friends if they want to give them

## What Parents Still Cover

- school uniform
- school shoes if needed for school
- sports kit required for school or clubs
- winter coat
- toiletries and household basics
- phone contract
- essential replacement clothing if something urgent is needed

## Core Principle

Allowance is regular and predictable.

Household responsibilities are expected and not separately paid.

Extra money can be earned only through clearly agreed extra jobs.

Extra screen time is not given casually. It must be budgeted, earned or bought under the rules below.

## Daily Expectations

These are non-negotiable expectations for living in a shared house.

- dirty clothes go in the laundry basket
- bags, shoes and kit are put away properly
- wrappers, cups, bowls and plates are cleared the same day
- no bowls, plates or glasses are left in bedrooms overnight
- no socks or clothing left on the floor
- bedroom floor stays clear enough to hoover
- each child clears their own mess from shared rooms every day

## Phone Time and Entertainment Screen Rules

### Core Principle

Phones are the main issue because they combine portability, constant access, notifications and highly addictive short-form content.

So the system should control:

- `phone time` most tightly
- `short-form video` most tightly of all
- while still counting laptop and solo TV entertainment in the wider picture

### Daily Base Formula

Phone time is the main budget. Social planning must be budgeted within that time, not treated as extra.

- `Daily phone time = 30 minutes + ((age - 10) x 30 minutes)`
- spreadsheet version: `=SUM((Age-10)*30+30)`

To support the transition to a more grown-up system, add a one-off daily bonus:

- `+30 minutes per day` for each child

That gives a starting daily phone budget of:

- Kit at 16: `210 minutes` (`3 hours 30 minutes`)
- Obie at 14: `150 minutes` (`2 hours 30 minutes`)

### What Counts

Count these against the daily phone time budget:

- TikTok
- Instagram Reels
- YouTube on phone
- games on phone
- entertainment browsing and scrolling
- social planning and messaging on phone

Do not normally count these against the phone time budget:

- maps
- school logistics
- banking or practical admin agreed by parents

Laptop and TV use should be controlled by a simpler rule:

- recreational laptop use and solo TV/YouTube should stay within a separate family-agreed entertainment limit
- if phone time has been used up, they should not automatically switch to laptop or TV as a loophole

Suggested practical rule:

- no more than `1 additional hour` of recreational laptop/TV entertainment on a normal school-day equivalent
- family films or shared watching can be treated separately

### Boundaries

- no TikTok, YouTube Shorts, Reels or other entertainment scrolling in the first hour after waking
- no TikTok, YouTube Shorts, Reels or other entertainment scrolling in the final hour before bed
- phones must be left charging downstairs overnight
- no extra time is added casually because time has been used up badly
- social planning does not unlock extra time and must be budgeted within the daily phone allowance
- no switching to laptop or TV as a workaround when phone entertainment time has run out

### Earning or Buying Extra Time

Extra phone time must be earned or bought, not argued for in the moment.

Examples:

- `15 minutes` for a small agreed extra job
- `30 minutes` for a larger agreed extra job
- extra time may also be bought from allowance at a rate agreed by parents

Suggested rate:

- `30 minutes = £1.50`

Parents can change this rate if it is being overused.

Unused daily phone time does not roll over unless parents explicitly agree to a one-off exception.

### Why This Structure Fits Current Research

This formula is based on a practical reading of current guidance and evidence, not on a single official number.

The most consistent themes in the research are:

- problematic or compulsive social media use appears more strongly linked to poor sleep and worse mental health than simple total screen time alone
- bedtime and overnight device access are particularly important because sleep disruption is one of the clearest harms
- short-form, autoplay and notification-heavy platforms are designed to keep attention and make stopping harder
- family plans, tech-free times and predictable boundaries are recommended more consistently than blanket bans
- messaging and social planning are valuable, but they can still expand to fill all available phone time unless they are budgeted

So this system is intentionally stricter about:

- phones
- short-form content
- first-hour and last-hour use
- overnight access
- random top-up requests

and more flexible about:

- shared TV use
- earned exceptions

## Behaviour Points System

This mirrors the school system because it is familiar and easy to understand.

### House Points

House points can be awarded for:

- doing jobs without being reminded
- showing initiative
- helping a sibling or parent without fuss
- budgeting well
- being reliable over several days

Suggested reward:

- every `5 house points` can be exchanged for either `30 minutes` extra screen time or `£2` extra allowance credit

### Behaviour Points

A behaviour point is given when a responsibility is missed, resisted or ignored.

Examples:

- bedroom or shared-space expectations not met by check time
- dishwasher or recycling rota job not done
- wrappers, bowls or cups left lying around
- clothes, socks, bags or kit left dumped after reminder
- pushing back, arguing or delaying instead of just doing the task
- not being ready on time when it affects the household

### Consequence Thresholds

- `1 behaviour point`: warning plus same-day correction
- `2 behaviour points in a week`: loss of `30 minutes` phone time the next day
- `3 behaviour points in a week`: detention equivalent

### Home Detention

At `3 behaviour points` in one week:

- they lose phone time for one evening
- they complete a household reset task before any leisure activity

Examples of detention tasks:

- full bedroom reset
- collect and clear all cups and bowls from upstairs and living spaces
- properly empty and reload dishwasher
- garden watering plus sweeping outside area

If behaviour continues after 3 points, each additional point can trigger:

- another `30 minutes` off screen time
- or a larger weekend consequence

## Allowance Protection and Allowance Deductions

Allowance should not be docked for every small daily issue. Screen time and behaviour points are the first-line consequence.

Allowance reduction should happen only if there is a repeated pattern across the week.

Suggested weekly rule:

- `0-1 behaviour points`: full allowance
- `2-3 behaviour points`: no allowance reduction, use screen-time consequence only
- `4-5 behaviour points`: `20%` reduction from next allowance payment
- `6+ behaviour points`: `50%` reduction from next allowance payment and family review

This keeps allowance tied to overall responsibility rather than individual moments of mess.

## Weekly Rota

Use a one-week-on, one-week-off rota.

### Week A

- Kit: dishwasher lead
- Obie: recycling and garden lead

### Week B

- Obie: dishwasher lead
- Kit: recycling and garden lead

### Dishwasher Lead

- empty dishwasher each morning before lunch
- help make sure dishes are stacked after dinner
- collect stray cups, bowls and plates from shared rooms by evening check

### Recycling and Garden Lead

- put recycling out on collection eve
- empty indoor recycling into main bins as needed
- water front and back gardens on agreed days
- do a quick check for outdoor clutter

## Daily Check Time

Set one fixed check time each day, for example `8:30pm`.

By check time:

- bedrooms must meet the agreed standard
- shared-room mess must be cleared
- rota jobs for that day must be done

If not, a behaviour point may be given and phone time pauses until the job is completed.

## Extra Paid Jobs

These are optional jobs above normal family expectations.

Examples:

- deep clean bathroom: `£3`
- mow lawn: `£3-£4`
- car clean: `£4`
- weeding or a bigger garden task: `£2-£4`
- sorting a cupboard or storage area: `£2-£3`

## Parent Rules

For this system to work, parents need to be consistent:

- do not add random extra screen time in the moment
- do not threaten consequences that will not be enforced
- record behaviour points simply and visibly
- keep consequences calm and boring
- praise reliability and initiative, not just final results

## Research Notes and Sources

These rules are informed by current official guidance and recent research:

- American Psychological Association, `Health Advisory on Social Media Use in Adolescence`:
  recommends limiting social media use where it interferes with sleep, physical activity or other healthy behaviours.
  Link: https://www.apa.org/topics/social-media-internet/health-advisory-adolescent-social-media-use

- U.S. Surgeon General, `Social Media and Youth Mental Health`:
  says we cannot conclude social media is sufficiently safe for children and adolescents; notes that more than `3 hours a day` of social media use is associated with about double the risk of poor mental health outcomes such as depression and anxiety; recommends family media plans and tech-free zones.
  Link: https://www.hhs.gov/surgeongeneral/reports-and-publications/youth-mental-health/social-media/index.html

- HealthyChildren.org / American Academy of Pediatrics, `How to Make a Family Media Plan`:
  recommends screen-free zones, one screen at a time, and turning off autoplay and notifications.
  Link: https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/How-to-Make-a-Family-Media-Use-Plan.aspx

- NHS / Gloucestershire Health and Care summary based on RCPCH guidance, `Screen Sense`:
  recommends at least `60 minutes` of screen-free time before bedtime and limiting screens in bedrooms at night.
  Link: https://www.ghc.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/sleep-and-screen-sense.pdf

- NHS Every Mind Matters, `Sleep`:
  notes that teenagers need more sleep than adults and that poor sleep is linked with low mood, irritability and concentration problems.
  Link: https://www.nhs.uk/every-mind-matters/mental-health-issues/sleep/

- Ofcom, `Children’s Media Use and Attitudes`:
  provides current UK evidence on how children and parents manage media use and is useful for keeping the household rules realistic.
  Link: https://www.ofcom.org.uk/media-use-and-attitudes/media-habits-children/childrens

- Ofcom `Children and Parents Media Use and Attitudes report 2025-6`:
  children aged 4-15 spent an average of `8 hours 45 minutes a week` watching video-sharing platforms in 2025, up around `16 minutes` on 2024; YouTube alone averaged `7 hours 7 minutes a week` across ages 4-15.
  Link: https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/research-and-data/media-literacy-research/children/2026-children-and-parents-report/children-and-parents-media-use-and-attitudes-report-2025-6.pdf?v=418231

- Ofcom `Children and parents: media use and attitudes report 2025` and the attached Ofcom slide the family reviewed:
  in 2024, children aged 4-15 spent `8 hours 29 minutes a week` on video-sharing platforms, and children aged 12-15 spent `6 hours 34 minutes a week` watching YouTube in the home.
  Link: https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/research-and-data/media-literacy-research/children/childrens-media-use-and-attitudes-report-2025/childrens-media-literacy-report-2025.pdf?v=396621

- Recent review evidence also suggests that `problematic social media use` is more strongly associated with poor sleep than general use alone, and that digital device use is linked with shorter sleep and later bedtimes.
  Example links:
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12840076/
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12564284/

## Six-Week Review Questions

At the review, ask:

- Are they managing their allowance sensibly?
- Are shared rooms staying clearer?
- Are there fewer arguments about screen time?
- Are chores getting done with fewer reminders?
- Does the allowance still feel fair given what they are expected to buy?

## Suggested Script for Introducing It

"We are changing the system now that you are older. You are getting more independence, more money and a bit more screen time, but also more responsibility. Your allowance is for budgeting and growing up, not for being paid to do every basic task. We expect you to contribute properly to the house, manage your own time better, and start paying for more of your own clothing and haircuts. We are also stopping random extra screen time. If you want more, you will need to earn it or buy it."
